# How I used n8n to automate my role (and turned it into a business opportunity)

[Discussion]

Ten months ago, I automated myself out of a job. Today I signed my first client.

I was working at a tech startup and put in charge of support engineering, ensuring customer issues were triaged and resolved by engineering in a timely manner, according to our internal SLA with the support team. I was the third person in this role, as it was notoriously difficult to manage, and I had to deal with a patchwork of inherited systems.

I was constantly firefighting. Chasing engineers for responses, missing SLA deadlines, and playing telephone between support and engineering teams.

My manager kept pushing for a tool purchase, but nothing fit our unique workflow. Plus, I had this nagging fear that if I automated my work, I'd automate myself out of a job.

When I finally tried n8n (which another team was already using), everything clicked. Within hours of tinkering, I could see possibilities I'd never imagined. The key unlock for me was that as a mostly self-taught developer, I could now build what I was envisioning without getting bogged down by low-level blockers like authentication or API formatting. I could create sophisticated automated workflows in hours instead of days.

I've noticed an interesting pattern since then. Seasoned developers often dismiss n8n because they're comfortable writing everything in code. But for someone at my skill level, it's the perfect bridge between having enough technical knowledge to understand what's happening and not being so deep in code that I overlook simpler solutions.

I spent a lot of time wrestling with the politics of automating my own job. The incentives felt completely misaligned. Why would I build myself out of work? In an ideal world, the company would recognize the initiative and let me apply that same problem-solving energy to other parts of the business, and I could move on to a bigger role. But I knew that was unlikely. Most companies just don't think that way, and it could be threatening for colleagues in other roles where I'd want to automate.

But my gut told me something different. Here I was, getting paid to learn a skill I was genuinely passionate about, in an area that was clearly valuable. Even if this company couldn't see the bigger picture, other companies would. Maybe I could even build something of my own eventually. I realized that staying in the status quo and being the human band-aid for broken processes would hurt me more than taking the risk. So I decided to lean in and see where it led.
